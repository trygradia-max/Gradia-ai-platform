You are the best detailer in your shop. You are also, most days, the receptionist, the estimator, the scheduler, and the accounts department. The problem isn't that you're bad at those jobs. It's that you can only do one of them at a time — and the one that pays is the one with your hands on the paint.

So the phone rings while you're three hours into a correction. You let it go. You tell yourself you'll call back at lunch. By lunch there are four more things on fire, and that caller has already booked with the shop across town that picked up on the second ring.

That missed call wasn't a missed call. It was a booked job that went somewhere else.

## The quiet math of a dropped lead

Detailers underestimate this number because it never shows up on a invoice. There's no line item for *revenue we didn't capture*. But run it honestly.

Say you miss three callable leads a week — conservative for a one-person shop in season. Say one in three would have booked, at an average ticket of $250. That's one lost job a week, $1,000 a month, **$12,000 a year** walking out the door before you ever quoted it. Add a ceramic or a PPF inquiry into that mix and the number stops being theoretical.

> The most expensive position in your shop isn't the one you pay the most. It's the one nobody's sitting in when a customer reaches out.

And it's not only the phone. It's the Instagram DM that sits unread for two days. The website form that goes to an inbox you check on Sundays. The text that comes in at 7pm when you're finally home. Every one of those is a person who decided, in that moment, to give you money — and then waited.

## "Just hire someone" is the wrong answer

The instinct is to hire a front desk. For most shops that math doesn't work. A part-time receptionist is $2,000–$3,000 a month, needs training on your menu and your prices, doesn't work nights or weekends, and still can't quote a ceramic job without checking with you.

You'd be paying a salary to solve a problem that's really about *coverage and consistency*, not headcount. You need the front desk to exist at 7pm on a Saturday. You need it to quote the same ceramic price you'd quote, every time, without inventing a number. You need it to remember that this caller emailed two days ago.

That's not a person problem. That's a systems problem.

## What "always-on" actually means

An AI office isn't a chatbot that deflects people with FAQs. Done right, it's coverage:

- The phone gets answered — in your shop's voice, quoting your real menu — even when you're under a car.
- The DM, the text, and the email all land in the same place, attached to the same customer.
- A booking gets proposed while the lead is still warm, not at lunch when the moment has passed.

The catch most shops worry about — *will it say something dumb and cost me a job?* — is the right thing to worry about. Which is exactly why the answer is **not** full autopilot. The office drafts the reply and the quote; you approve it with one tap. The speed of an instant response, with your judgment still on the trigger.

## Start where it bleeds

You don't have to automate your whole operation to stop the bleeding. Pick the channel that loses you the most: for most shops it's the phone, for some it's DMs. Cover that one. Watch what comes back.

The goal was never to replace the craft. It's to stop letting the craft cost you the customers who wanted to pay for it.
